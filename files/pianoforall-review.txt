Piano For All is a piano course for adults who never learned to read music, sold as nine parts of video lessons for a single payment. It has been online since 2006, which is long enough that most of the reviews you will find were written by people who bought it years ago.

What makes it interesting now is the price model rather than the method. Most piano teaching has moved to apps that charge monthly, and this does not: $49 once, access for life, nothing renews. Whether that is a bargain depends on one thing the app makers sell hard and this course cannot offer — feedback on whether you are actually playing the right notes.

Everything below was checked on 28 November 2026, including the checkout.

## What $49 actually buys

Nine parts, in this order: Party Time (rhythm-style piano), Blues & Rock 'n' Roll, Chord Magic (inversions and chord changes at speed), Advanced Chords Made Easy, Ballad Style & Improvisation, Jazz Piano Made Easy, Advanced Blues Piano, Taming the Classics, and Speed Learning.

The delivery is two things at once. There is an online course player — the company calls it the Academy — that tracks your place and works on a phone, tablet or computer. And there are downloadable ebooks with the video and audio embedded in the file, so a lesson works offline on a laptop or an iPad with no connection. That offline copy is the part app subscribers never get: stop paying an app and the library disappears; here the files are on your drive.

Around the lessons you get a student community area and direct email access to Robin Hall, the teacher who made the course. He has thirty years of teaching behind it and answers support himself, according to the site.

The purchase is a one-time payment with lifetime access, including future updates. There is no subscription, no trial that converts, and no renewal date.

## The method is a deliberate order of operations

Part 1 starts with chord shapes and fifteen popular rhythm patterns, played with both hands together. You are making recognisable music in the first session, or that is the claim, and notation is not introduced until later parts.

The reasoning is that reading music is a decoding skill that stalls adult beginners for months, while chord patterns get them playing songs in days. Reading is introduced alongside pieces you can already play, so the notation explains something you have already heard yourself make.

It is a real pedagogical position and it has a cost. If your goal is to sight-read Chopin from a score, this teaches you to read, but it teaches you late and gently. Part 8, Taming the Classics, is where notation gets serious.

## What you cannot see before you pay

The home page says you can watch a lesson before spending a penny. On the three pages we loaded — the home page, the course page and the pricing page — no lesson video was embedded or linked. What is inspectable before paying is the full curriculum, a paragraph describing each of the nine parts, the FAQ, the teacher's biography and the student reviews.

That is more than most paid course pages show, and it is still not a lesson. You cannot judge the teaching style, the camera work or whether Hall's explanations land with you until after checkout. The 90-day refund window is the mechanism that covers that gap, and it is the reason a course with no preview is still a reasonable thing to buy — provided you actually use the window rather than filing the purchase away for a rainy month that never comes.

## Where an app beats it, plainly

Skoove and Piano Marvel both listen to you play. Connect a digital keyboard by USB or let the microphone hear an acoustic piano, and the app marks the note you hit, in time, on screen. That is the single feature this course cannot match, because it is recorded video: you play along and nothing tells you that you were a beat late or a semitone flat.

For a beginner, that feedback is worth something real. It is also the thing that turns practice into a game and keeps people coming back, which is why the apps charge monthly and keep charging.

Two honest counterweights. First, feedback that measures note accuracy does not teach taste, accompaniment patterns or improvisation — the things this course spends eight of its nine parts on. Second, the apps' monthly fee never stops, and a course you own outright costs nothing to return to in three years.

## Who it suits, and who should not buy it

It suits an adult beginner who wants to sit down and play recognisable songs — pop, blues, ballads, a bit of jazz — and who is happy to be their own judge. It suits someone returning to piano after decades away and starting from patterns rather than scales. It suits anyone who dislikes subscriptions on principle, and anyone who practices where there is no reliable internet, because the ebooks work on a plane.

Do not buy it if you want to be corrected. If wrong notes go unnoticed for a week and you cannot hear the difference yet, an app that lights up the keyboard will move you faster than any video. And do not buy it if your goal is formal classical training with a graded syllabus — Part 8 will not replace a teacher for that.

## Final thoughts

For $49 the question is not whether Piano For All is the best way to learn piano. It is whether a well-structured, thirty-year teaching method you own outright is worth more to you than a cheaper-to-start app that hears your mistakes.

If you will practice on your own and you want songs rather than exercises, this is the better buy, and the offline files make it the more durable one. If you know yourself well enough to admit that you need something to tell you when you are wrong, pay Skoove monthly instead and come back to this when you can hear it yourself.
