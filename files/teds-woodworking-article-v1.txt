## What free plans really cost

The case for free plans is not that they are worse. Plenty are excellent — published by lumber yards, hobbyist communities and retired tradespeople who have no product to sell. The case is that you pay in something other than money:
- Search time. Finding a plan that matches the size you need, in a format you can read, takes longer than building it some weekends.
- Missing cut lists. A lot of free plans are a drawing and a hope. The materials list and cut list are the difference between a Saturday and three trips to the hardware store.
- Scale and unit errors. Plans re-uploaded through image editors, or posted with no stated units, are common and expensive when they go wrong.
- No one to ask. When step four does not make sense, there is nobody on the other end.
- Dead links. Plan archives move, and the project you bookmarked two years ago is now a 404.

None of that is an argument for paying. It is an argument for knowing what you are buying when you do.

## Where it stops being worth it
- You have one project in mind. A workbench, a shelf, a doghouse. Free plans for the common projects are abundant and often better than a generic library entry, because somebody built that exact one and wrote it up.
- You enjoy designing. If you sketch your own pieces, a plan library is dead weight.
- You need a specific size. A plan for a 1,800 mm bench is not much help if your alcove is 1,640 mm. The custom request may cover this, but it is a promise, not a guarantee.
- You need to be sure the plans are good. This is the big one, and we are not going to paper over it: from outside the paywall, nobody can verify the quality of 16,000 individual plans. The sales page shows samples, and they are the seller’s pick — normal, but it means you are judging the best of it.

## Before you pay, do these four things
1. Read the checkout total. Checkouts for offers like this can carry order bumps and currency conversion. The $67 on the page is not always the number on the card statement.
2. Screenshot the guarantee. The page promises 60 days and a 48-hour refund. Save the page and note the date, so the terms you agreed to are the terms you can point to.
3. Check the physical-copies price. “Option to have everything on physical copies” is an extra charge with shipping. If you want paper, price it before you commit.
4. Confirm the file formats suit you. PDF plans and a DWG/CAD viewer are fine on a laptop; awkward on a phone in a shed.

## Verdict

Free plans win for the one-off project, for anyone still learning to read a drawing, and for anyone who enjoys the design part as much as the building. There is no version of this where paying $67 for a workbench plan is sensible.

Teds Woodworking is defensible for the person who builds regularly, wants cut lists and a single searchable index, and accepts that plan quality across a library that size is a lottery they cannot inspect first. The 60-day refund is the thing that makes that gamble survivable: it is long enough to open twenty plans and see whether the format and detail level suit you.

What we would not do is buy it as a beginner, expecting a course. It is a plan library, not tuition. If you need to be taught, the money is better spent on a structured course with a progression, and free plans are a perfectly good place to practise in the meantime.
