## The verdict first

If you build several projects a year and want one searchable place where the plan you need already exists, with a cut list attached, Teds Woodworking is a defensible $67. What you are buying is access to a large library, not a promise that every plan in it is worth building.

If you have one project in mind, or you are new enough that you want to be taught rather than handed a drawing, don't buy it. A genuinely complete free plan exists and costs nothing; we walk through one below.

## Where the arithmetic goes soft

The page explains how it arrived at 16,000. It is worth reading closely, because the numbers it gives do not add up to the number in the headline.

The explanation runs: “a team of twelve. Full-time… Five new plans every week. Fifty weeks a year. Over two decades.” Five plans a week across fifty weeks is 250 a year. Twenty years of that is 5,000. Twenty-five years, which is the tenure used elsewhere on the page, gets you to 6,250. Reaching 16,000 at that rate would take about sixty-four years.

A few sections later the page describes the monthly drop differently: “Every month, our workshop team builds 5-8 new projects from our workshop classes.” That is 60 to 96 a year, not 250.

Two honest readings are possible. Either the team has been larger in the past than the twelve described today, or the library includes plans acquired rather than built in-house. The page does not say which. What it does say is that every plan was built and tested by that team, and that is the claim the arithmetic strains against.

In fairness, the category counts are internally consistent. They add to 16,050, which is close enough to the headline to look deliberate rather than invented. The problem is not that the number is obviously false. It is that you cannot check it before paying, and the page's own explanation of it does not survive a calculator.

## What a complete free plan actually looks like

The usual argument for paying is that free plans are drawings with no measurements. That is sometimes true and sometimes the opposite of true, so here is a specific one instead of a generalisation.

Ana White's 2x4 Outdoor Sofa is free, no account needed, and it includes:
- A shopping list with quantities: 11 × 2x4x10′, 3 deep seat cushions at 24″ × 24″, and 150 exterior decking screws at 2-1/2″ to 3″.
- A cut list with exact dimensions: 9 × 2x4 at 72″, 13 × 2x4 at 25-1/2″, 2 × 2x4 at 28-1/2″. It also explains how to adjust each one for a different cushion size, which is more useful than a fixed number.
- A tool list: measuring tape, square, pencil, drill, compound mitre saw. Five items, all ordinary.
- Eight numbered build steps with a diagram for each, then finishing instructions for paint, interior stain and exterior stain.
- A metric toggle, so the same plan reads in millimetres.
- 332 reader builds listed on the page, which is a form of evidence that the plan works in other people's garages.

That is a complete plan. It is also, obviously, one plan. It is a furniture plan built from standard lumber, and it will not help you build a shed, a chicken coop or a router jig. Ana White's catalogue lists 1,761 plans as of 26 November 2026, mostly furniture and home builds, and the site also sells ad-free PDF bundles (six of those outdoor-furniture plans for $6) for people who want a tidy printable version.

The useful comparison is not free versus paid. It is a well-made plan versus a badly made one, at any price. Free libraries have both. So do paid ones.

## Where the library is strong, and where it is weak

Strong: the format. One search box, one set of categories, one difficulty rating, instead of a dozen browser tabs and a Pinterest board. If you price your own time at anything, that is what the money buys. The breadth is real too. A free furniture library will not hand you a chicken coop, a deer stand and a router table in the same place, and this one claims to.

Weak: you cannot see it first. The sales page publishes a handful of screenshots showing a dimensioned blueprint, a cutting list and step-by-step panels. Those screenshots look like real working drawings, and they are the seller's choice of what to show you. Beyond that, everything about quality inside the members area is unverified until you have paid.

That is the whole gamble, and it is not a small one. A library that size, assembled over twenty-five years, will contain plans of wildly different ages and standards. The 180-day return is what makes the gamble survivable: long enough to open twenty plans, look at how they are drawn, and decide whether the house style suits you.

## Which one suits you

You have a specific project in mind. Search for a free plan first, and look at what it gives you. If it has a cut list, a materials list and dimensioned drawings, you are done and you have saved $67. This is the most common case and the least interesting one to be sold.

You are starting out and want to learn. A plan library is not tuition. It will not teach you to read a drawing, set up a saw or fix a joint that closed up. Buy a course, or work through free plans until the drawings make sense. The library becomes useful after that, not before.

You build often, across different kinds of project, and you are tired of searching. This is who the product is for. If you would spend an evening hunting for a plan more than twice a year, and you want the cut list in a consistent format when you find it, the maths works in your favour. Twenty projects bought individually at Woodsmith prices would cost more than the library does.

## The recommendation

Buy it if the searching is the part you resent, you build regularly, and you are comfortable paying NZ$156 to find out whether the plans are drawn the way you like them, with 180 days to change your mind.

Do not buy it as a beginner, as a substitute for learning, or to solve one project. In those cases the free option is not a compromise, it is the better answer, and the 2x4 sofa above is the proof: a shopping list, a cut list, a tool list and eight illustrated steps, for nothing.

If you do want to look at the current offer, the seller's page and the checkout are here. Read the total in your own currency before you enter card details, and leave the three add-on boxes alone unless you actually want those books.
