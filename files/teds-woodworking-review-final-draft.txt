What Teds Woodworking actually is

It is a one-time purchase that unlocks an online members area: 16,000 woodworking plans, searchable by keyword, filed into ten categories. That is the pitch. There is no app, no course and nothing shipped unless you pay extra for printed copies.

The promise the whole thing rests on is what one plan contains: step-by-step instructions, a cutting list, a materials list and dimensioned drawings. If those four things are good, $67 is cheap. If they aren't, a large library is a large filing cabinet.

You are promised 50 plans and a 440-page book. What arrives is one 114 MB PDF, 440 pages, hosted on the seller's own server with no login required. Its cover says 40 step-by-step plans. The remaining plans are dripped by email over ten days.

This offer's checkout says 180 days. The sales page says 60 days and promises refunds within 48 hours. Three numbers, one purchase.

On digital products, ClickBank can refund less than the full price.

Serial returners can be blocked from buying again.

Payment method matters too: a Klarna purchase follows Klarna's timelines, not card rules. And the 48-hour claim is the seller's — nothing in the policy promises a turnaround. If you buy, screenshot the checkout terms with the date visible.

Does 16,000 add up?

The page explains the number itself: “a team of twelve. Full-time… Five new plans every week. Fifty weeks a year. Over two decades.”

Five a week across fifty weeks is 250 a year — 5,000 over twenty years, 6,250 over twenty-five. Reaching 16,000 at that rate takes about sixty-four years. Elsewhere the same page says the team builds “5-8 new projects” a month, which is 60 to 96 a year.

The category counts do add to 16,050, so the headline is internally consistent. Two innocent explanations fit and the page never settles which: a larger team in earlier years, or a library that includes plans the current team didn't draw. Neither is dishonest. But the page's own explanation of the number doesn't produce the number, and no buyer can count 16,000 plans afterwards.

Who should buy it

You build often, across different project types. Furniture this month, a shed next. If you'd spend an evening hunting for a plan more than twice a year, $67 for one indexed library is cheaper than your time. Twenty projects at Woodsmith prices is $260–$340.

You want the cut list in the same place every time. That consistency is the real product. If you've got forty PDFs from forty sites, you already know why that matters.

Who shouldn't

You have one project in mind. Search free first. If the plan you find has a cut list, a materials list and dimensions, you're done and you've kept $67.

You're learning. A library assumes you can read a drawing and cut to a line. 16,000 drawings won't teach you that; a free plan and a stack of scrap will.

You want to see what you're buying. There is no way to do that here. If that matters — and for $67 it reasonably might — don't buy it.

The verdict

Insufficient Evidence. The offer is real, the price is clear, and the refund window is long enough to test the thing properly. None of that is the question.

The question is whether 16,000 indexed plans are worth $67 to you, and there is no way to find out without paying — no demo, no trial, and a free sample that is a 2008 book rather than a look inside the library. We won't call that recommended.

If you build regularly and want one place to look, the terms are fair enough to try, and the checkout gives you 180 days to ask for a refund. If you need a plan this weekend, take Ana White's sofa or Woodsmith's free deck planter bench and spend nothing.
