What the refund terms actually say

This is the part most reviews get wrong, so here is the detail instead of a promise.

ClickBank's published Return and Subscription Cancellation Policy, dated 26 August 2026, sets its own baseline at 60 days: ClickBank will “at its discretion, allow for the return or replacement of any product within 60 days from the date of purchase”. It then says sellers may offer return policies outside that window, and that buyers should read the seller's policy for the full terms.

The checkout for this product displays a 180-day window. The sales page advertises 60 days and says refunds are handled within 48 hours of an email. So the seller's own funnel gives two different windows, and the policy document names a third figure. We cannot establish from outside which one a particular request would be assessed under.

Three things in the policy do apply regardless of which number you land on:

Returns are described as being at ClickBank's discretion, not an automatic entitlement.

For digital products, ClickBank reserves the right to refund less than the full purchase price.

Customers making serial or repeated returns can be blocked from buying again.

The policy also folds in payment-method rules — where a purchase is made through Klarna, for example, the refund follows Klarna's process and timelines rather than card rules. And the 48-hour processing claim on the sales page is the seller's; nothing in ClickBank's policy promises a turnaround.

The practical reading: 60 days is the number named in the retailer's policy, 180 is the number printed on this offer's checkout, and both are discretionary. If you buy, screenshot the checkout terms with the date visible. That turns a marketing claim into something you can point to later.

What the library won't do for you

It won't let you try before you buy. The free pack is a book; the members area is the product. A few sales-page screenshots and a scanned guide do not show you how 16,000 plans are indexed, how current they are, or how consistent they are with each other.

It isn't tuition. A plan assumes you can read a drawing, set up a saw and cut to a line. If you can't yet, a library of 16,000 drawings won't fix that.

Size isn't consistency. A collection assembled over a long period, by a team of unknown size, will contain plans of different ages and standards. The seller says every plan was built and tested before release. Across a catalogue that size we can't check it, and a large number is not evidence of even quality. The ten category counts do add to 16,050, which suggests deliberate curation rather than a headline rounded up — but curated counts still don't tell you whether plan 12,441 is any good.

Who this suits, and who it doesn't

Have one project in mind? Search free first and read what the plan gives you. If it has a cut list, a materials list and dimensioned drawings, you're done and you've saved $67. That is the most common case and the least interesting one to be sold.

Starting out and wanting to learn? Don't buy this yet. Work through free plans until drawings make sense, then come back if you still want an index. The library is useful after you can build.

Building often, across different kinds of project, tired of hunting? This is the reader the product is aimed at, and the one case where the terms are worth considering. If you'd spend an evening looking for a plan more than twice a year, and want the cut list in a consistent place when you find it, twenty projects at Woodsmith prices would cost more than the library does.

Does the 16,000 add up?

This doesn't decide whether the library is useful, but it is worth knowing before you pay for a number you can't count yourself.

The page explains where the figure came from: “a team of twelve. Full-time… Five new plans every week. Fifty weeks a year. Over two decades.” Five plans a week across fifty weeks is 250 a year, which is 5,000 over twenty years and 6,250 over twenty-five. Reaching 16,000 at that rate takes about sixty-four years.

Further down, the monthly drop is described differently: “Every month, our workshop team builds 5-8 new projects.” That is 60 to 96 a year.

Two ordinary explanations fit and the page doesn't settle which. The team may have been larger in earlier years than the twelve described today, or the library may include plans the current team didn't draft. Neither is dishonest, and the category counts do add to 16,050, which looks deliberate rather than invented.

So this is a question, not a finding. The advertised count may well be accurate. What we can say is that the page's own explanation of it doesn't produce the number, and that we could not independently verify the advertised total without purchasing access.

The verdict, in one place

Insufficient Evidence, for a narrow and specific reason: the purchase is the members area, and nobody outside it has seen the members area — including us.

What we did verify: $67.00 with no tax added in the US checkout, a long but inconsistently stated refund window, a free pack that is a scanned 2008-era book rather than a sample of the library, and a headline plan count whose own production rate doesn't reach it.

What remains unknown is the part that decides the purchase: whether the plans are drawn to a consistent standard, whether the search works, and whether an index over 16,000 plans is worth $67 to you.

If you build regularly and want one place to look, the terms are reasonable enough to try, and the refund window is long enough to test the thing properly in the first fortnight. If you need a plan this weekend, take Ana White's sofa, or Woodsmith's free deck planter bench for a professionally drawn one, and spend nothing.

If you buy before we do, do it deliberately: read the total, leave the add-on boxes alone unless you want those books, and keep the checkout page.
