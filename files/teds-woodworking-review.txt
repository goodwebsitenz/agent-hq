Ted's Woodworking is a one-time $67 library of woodworking plans — 16,000 of them, according to the company — aimed at the point where you have a project in mind and no plan detailed enough to build it. The free plan you found shows the finished table and skips the joinery. The measurements assume milled lumber you don't have. One wrong cut turns $60 of boards into firewood.

A library that closes that gap is worth paying for; one that doesn't is a filing cabinet. So the questions that matter are what's in it, what a plan is meant to contain, and how it compares with the plans you can already get for free.

## What Ted's Woodworking is

It's an online members area rather than an app, a course or a box of printed plans. You pay once, get a login, and download as many plans as you like for as long as the site runs. Lifetime access, nothing renewing.

The library is divided into ten categories — furniture, storage, outdoor and garden, buildings and shelters, animal and pet, home decor, kitchen and dining, kids and recreation, workshop and tools, specialty. Inside, you can search by keyword, browse by category and filter by difficulty, so "garden bench, beginner" is a search rather than a scroll through folders.

## What you get
- The library. 16,000 plans and projects, on the company's own count. The category breakdown adds up to 16,050, so the number is at least internally consistent.
- Plans built for building. Advertised as step-by-step instructions, exact cut lists, materials lists and dimensioned drawings with exploded views of the joints — the four things that separate a plan from a picture.
- Search and filters. Keyword search, category browsing and beginner/intermediate/advanced levels.
- Ongoing additions. New plans added monthly, included in the membership at no extra charge.
- A custom plan request. Describe a project the library doesn't cover and Ted's team drafts one for you. The page values it at $250.
- Four bonuses. A DWG/CAD plan viewer ($47), 150 woodworking videos ($77), a guide to starting a woodworking business ($27) and a set of woodworking guides ($39) — values as stated on the site.
- Delivery. Instant access to the members area, or DVDs and a USB drive shipped for an additional fee.

## What it does well

### One payment, and everything is yours to keep

$67.00 buys the library. We opened the US checkout on 27 November 2026: subtotal $67.00, tax $0.00, total $67.00, one line item, nothing recurring.

That suits woodworking better than most hobbies, because building comes in bursts. A winter of projects followed by eight months with the tools under a sheet is normal, and a subscription bills you through the quiet months. If you expect to still be building in a few years, the maths is simple.

### The whole library is one index, not a shop

Most paid plans are sold one project at a time — Woodsmith's run $9.95 to $16.95 each. That works when you know exactly what you're building. It's slow and expensive when you don't, because every idea starts with a decision about whether it's worth another purchase.

Here the buying and the browsing are separate events: once you've paid, looking costs nothing but time. That's the main practical argument for a library over a plan shop.

### A plan drawn for your space

The custom plan request is the one thing no catalogue or free site can match. A corner cabinet for a 34-inch gap under a sloping ceiling doesn't exist in anyone's library, and no free site or plan shop will draw one for you. How good the drafted plan is depends on the team behind it, and we can't judge that from outside — but the option itself is genuinely unusual at this price.

### It keeps growing

New plans arrive monthly at no extra cost, which turns a static archive into something that keeps earning its place — a jig in March, a Christmas project in November. A library that expands is worth more in year three than in week one, and that is the period this price is built for.

Where Ted's leads. Range, plainly: the library is many times the size of anything else in the table, and neither Ana White nor Woodsmith will draft a plan for your particular alcove. A single payment is also the only route here to owning a large library outright rather than renting access to one.

Where Woodsmith is better. Disclosure. You can see a plan's length, illustration count and contents before you pay, which tells you what the money buys. Their barn-door cabinet is $16.95 for 14 pages of instructions and more than 90 illustrations and exploded views. If judging a plan's quality before purchase matters to you, individual plans are the safer buy — and $16.95 for one project you actually want is not bad value.

Where Ana White wins. The obvious one. Her catalogue is free, 1,761 plans deep, and a plan we opened had a dimensions diagram, a shopping list, an exact cut list and seven illustrated steps. If your project is a farmhouse table or a toy box, there's a good chance it's already there. What you won't get is anything custom, or a plan for a project nobody has published.

The three aren't interchangeable per plan, so read this as a comparison of what you're buying rather than a price-per-plan ranking.

## Who it's for

Someone who builds regularly across different projects. A bench this month, a bookcase next, a planter box for a birthday. Paying per project taxes that pattern; one index removes it.

Someone whose project isn't in any catalogue. A built-in for an awkward alcove, a desk for a specific wall, a cabinet sized to a fridge. The custom request is the reason to be here rather than anywhere else.

Someone who wants to stop hunting and start cutting. If your weekends go on plans that turn out to be missing half the measurements, the value is the hours back.

If you have one common project in mind, this isn't the right first purchase. Free plans cover the popular builds well, and a single Woodsmith plan gives you far more visible detail for under $17.
