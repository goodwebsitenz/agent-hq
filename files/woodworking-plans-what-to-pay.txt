Woodworking plans are sold in three shapes: free, by the project, and by the library. The price runs from nothing to $67, and the difference between them is not really the number of plans — it is what is on the page when you sit down to cut.

Short answer: if you have one project in mind, a free plan is usually enough and a $67 library is the wrong first purchase. A library earns its price when you build often enough that hunting for a plan becomes the bottleneck, or when the project you want doesn't exist anywhere free.

To test that, we took one bench — a simple outdoor piece with angled legs — and looked at what each route actually hands you for the same job. Everything below was checked on 28 November 2026.

## What a usable plan has to contain

Four things separate a plan from a picture of furniture. A shopping list tells you what to buy. A cut list tells you what to cut it into, with the angles written down. Dimensioned drawings show how the parts sit together. And step photographs show the order, because joinery order is where most first builds go wrong.

Missing any one of those and you are improvising — which is fine if you have built before and miserable if you haven't. Free plans vary wildly on this. Some are a sketch and a shopping list; some are better documented than what people pay for. The only way to know is to open one and look, which is why "how much can you inspect before paying" is the first question in the table further down.

## One bench, three sources

Free — Ana White, Homestead Bench. The plan is a single web page, no login, no email. It gives a shopping list of three 8-foot 2x4s, one 10-foot 2x8, 2-3/4" self-tapping screws and a finish. The cut list is the part that matters: it gives each piece by length and specifies that both ends come off at 10 degrees, and whether the ends end up parallel or not — the detail most free plans leave out and the one that ruins a bench if you guess. There is a metric toggle that converts the shopping and cut lists to millimetres, and fifteen illustrated steps.

$10.95 — Woodsmith, Porch Bench. A paid single plan, and the page tells you exactly what you get before you pay: seven printable pages of step-by-step instructions, twenty-seven full-colour photos, illustrations and exploded views, a materials list, and links to the supplies and hardware. The design has mortise-and-tenon joinery rather than screws, which is a better bench and a harder build.

$67 — Teds Woodworking. A library of 16,000 plans on the company's own count, across ten categories including outdoor and garden. There is no preview: you cannot open a single plan, count its pages or see its cut list until you have paid. What you are buying is the index and the odds that your project is in it — a bench almost certainly is, but you won't see the drawing until after checkout. We could not inspect one, so we can't tell you how a Teds bench plan compares with the two above.

## Three checks before you cut

Whichever route you took, the same three things decide whether the plan is usable. Does the cut list give every length and every angle, or only the lengths? Do the drawings show how two parts meet, or only where they end up? And does the materials list add up to the cut list — a plan that asks for three 8-foot boards and then cuts 26 feet of parts has an error in it, and you find out at the saw.

## When free is enough, and when it isn't

Buy nothing if you have a common project. Farmhouse table, bench, planter box, bookshelf — these exist free, and some of them are documented better than paid plans. Ana White's catalogue alone is 1,761 plans. Woodsmith also publishes a free section; the first page lists 12.

Pay $10.95 when you want a specific piece done properly. Woodsmith's bench has angled joinery, concealed mortise and tenon, and twenty-seven drawings and photographs behind it. If the bench matters and you want the joinery explained rather than approximated, ten dollars is the cheapest part of the build.

Pay $67 when the search is the problem. If you build most weekends, or your project is unusual enough that three free sites don't have it, a single index you can search beats opening forty tabs. That is the case for the library, and it is the only one that holds up. If you want the full breakdown of what that $67 includes, we reviewed it separately, plan by plan.

Pay $59.95 a year if you want the magazine too. Unlimited bundles website access with the print archive and back issues. Over a few years it costs more than a one-time library and it renews, but it also carries forty years of ShopNotes and Woodsmith, which no one else has.

## What we could not check

We have not bought the Teds library, so we cannot tell you whether a Teds bench plan carries a cut list as precise as the free one above, or drawings as complete as Woodsmith's. That is the whole risk of a no-preview library: the sample that would let you judge it is the thing you pay to see. We also could not count the 16,000 files; the ten categories reconcile with the headline, but that is arithmetic, not inspection.

## The decision

For one bench, the free plan is the better plan — it is complete, it is dimensioned, and the cut list carries the angles. Woodsmith's $10.95 buys visibly more drawing for a project you care about. Neither is a reason to spend $67.

The library is a different purchase: it buys the end of searching, and it is priced for someone who builds repeatedly. If that is you, it is the cheapest way to own a large catalogue outright; if it isn't, buy the $10.95 plan and put the rest towards lumber.
